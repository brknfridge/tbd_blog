Every founder has sat through the 4Ps of marketing at some point — Product, Price, Place, Promotion. It gets taught in a classroom, filed away as "marketing theory," and forgotten the moment the business gets real. That's a mistake. The 4Ps were never just a framework for writing marketing plans. They're four distinct growth centres — four separate places you can go looking for growth when the obvious lever, spending more on ads, stops working or stops being affordable.

Most founders only ever pull on one of these four levers. Here's how to think about all of them.

## 01 — Product

The most under-used growth lever is the product itself, mostly because founders assume the product is "done" once it ships. It never is.

**Differentiation** is the first place to look. Not differentiation as a slogan, but as an honest answer to "why you, and not the ten other options in this category." If you can't answer that in one sentence, customers can't either, and you're competing on price by default.

**New product development** is the second. Growth doesn't only come from selling more of what you have — it comes from giving existing customers a reason to buy again, and giving new customers a wider door to walk through. A second SKU, a smaller size, a bundle — these aren't distractions from the core business, they're often how the core business grows.

**Doubling down on winners** is the lever founders resist the most, because it feels like admitting the rest of the catalogue isn't working. But most brands find that a small number of products drive most of the revenue. Once you know which ones, the growth question becomes simple: are you putting your best merchandising, best content, and best ad spend behind them — or splitting it evenly across everything?

Sitting quietly under all of this is **packaging**, which is really a repackaging of value, not just a box. Think of Nespresso pods versus a bag of ground coffee. It's the same coffee, roughly. But the pod format changes the price you can charge, the frequency of purchase, the machine lock-in, and the entire category the product competes in. Packaging isn't decoration — it's a growth decision disguised as a design decision.

## 02 — Pricing

Founders tend to treat pricing as a lever with exactly one setting: discount. If growth slows, run a promotion. This is usually the most expensive way to grow, because it trains customers to wait for the next one and erodes the price you can charge everyone else.

The better way to think about pricing is as a **value proposition** question, not a discounting question. There are two kinds of value you're pricing against.

**Economic value** is value you can calculate. Take O-rings that last twice as long as the standard ones on the market. They can be priced well above the standard product, not because of branding, but because the customer's total cost of ownership goes down — fewer replacements, less downtime, less labour. When you can show the maths, price becomes a rational decision for the buyer, not an emotional one.

**Perceived value** is the harder, and often bigger, lever. It's not calculated — it's felt. Take a brand like Gully Labs, a homegrown sneaker label. Two years ago, it read as a scrappy, budget-friendly alternative to international sneaker brands. Today, through sharper design, better storytelling, and visible community credibility, it reads as a brand people choose on its own merits, not as a compromise. The product category didn't change. The perception of what it's worth did — and that perception is what let pricing move up without losing customers.

Discounts and promotions have their place. But they should be the last lever you pull on price, not the first.

## 03 — Promotion

This is the lever every founder is already pulling, usually harder than any other. But "run more ads" is not a strategy — it's an instruction. Two things separate promotion that compounds from promotion that just burns budget.

The first is picking **advertising channels relevant to your target group**, not the channels that are cheapest or most talked about. A B2B founder chasing TikTok trends because a D2C brand went viral there is optimising for the wrong audience. The question isn't "where is attention cheap right now," it's "where does my specific customer actually spend their time, and what are they doing there."

The second is **creative built for funnel stage**. A cold audience that has never heard of you needs a completely different message than a warm audience that has visited your site three times and abandoned a cart. Running the same ad creative across every stage of the funnel is one of the most common — and most expensive — mistakes founders make with their media spend. Top-of-funnel creative should educate and earn attention. Bottom-of-funnel creative should remove the last objection standing between browsing and buying.

## 04 — Distribution

The fourth "P" doesn't even start with a P — Place — but the growth centre it represents is distribution, and it's usually where the biggest untapped growth sits.

**Channel expansion** means going beyond wherever you currently sell. If you're only on your own website, marketplaces like Amazon or Flipkart open you up to customers who were never going to search for your brand directly. If you're only online, offline retail or quick-commerce apps open a different kind of impulse buyer. Each new channel comes with its own economics and its own headaches, but each one also removes a reason a potential customer didn't buy from you.

**Geo expansion and penetration** is the other half. Expansion means entering new markets or cities you don't currently serve. Penetration means going deeper into markets you already sell into but haven't fully saturated — better local marketing, faster delivery, more visible presence. Founders often chase expansion because it feels like growth, while penetration, the less glamorous option, is usually cheaper and faster to execute.

---

None of these four levers work in isolation, and none of them replace the others. A great product with bad distribution never gets discovered. Great promotion for a poorly differentiated product just accelerates how quickly customers figure out there's nothing special there. The founders who grow well aren't the ones who found one lever that works — they're the ones who know all four exist, and know which one to pull when the others stop moving.
